BRIEF A Nash equilibrium is a state where no player can improve their outcome by changing strategy alone, given everyone else's strategy stays fixed.
The Prisoner's Dilemma is the standard proof of this — the equilibrium is worse for both players than if they'd cooperated.
· ·
CONCEPT INDEX
Best response · a strategy that maximises payoff given the others Dominant strategy · best regardless of what others do Mixed strategy · randomising between options Pareto efficiency · no one can gain without someone losing Payoff matrix · the table of outcomes for every strategy pair
DETAILED NOTES Both betraying is the Nash equilibrium — neither player can improve by unilaterally switching — even though mutual silence leaves both better off.

Flowchart diagram — strategy/best-response loop
Read the matrix from one player's side at a time. Fix the column at Stay Silent: betraying gives 0 years against 1, so betraying wins. Fix it at Betray: betraying gives 5 against 10, so betraying wins again. Betrayal is a dominant strategy for both, and the pair of dominant strategies is the equilibrium. The result generalises past two players. Any finite game with a fixed number of players and strategies has at least one equilibrium once mixed strategies are allowed — Nash's 1950 proof, which is why the concept became the default lens for bargaining, auctions, arms races and pricing. Where it breaks down is worth knowing. Equilibria are not necessarily unique, not necessarily efficient, and say nothing about how players would find one. Repeated play changes the picture entirely: cooperation becomes sustainable once defection can be punished in later rounds.
WORKED EXAMPLE Two petrol stations across the road from each other. Both at £1.40 is an equilibrium: undercutting to £1.35 wins volume for a day, the other matches, and both end up earning less on the same customers.
